Seeing Graphics Clearly

Direct perception, graphical structure, and better judgment calls for scientific figures

Josh M. Pollock · adapted from PyData 2026

Today I want to talk about two things. First is a way to see what a graphic is doing before judging it by chart type. The second is GoFish, a tool for building graphics out of those visible relationships.

start with the lakes

The examples use fishery data: lakes, species, catch amounts, and locations.

That matters because the data are naturally relational. We care about parts and wholes, where things happened, and how the same species moves through several views.

This is the SciPy setup slide. The data are simple enough to keep in your head, but rich enough to show composition, grouping, containment, connection, and emphasis.

how do we decide what chart to use?

Now how do we decide what chart to use today? Maybe we are lucky to have some existing collection of charts we know work well. Maybe we can vibe code our way to something that looks alright. But when we're faced with a new problem what should we do?
We could sift through giant galleries and books of cool examples, seeing if any of them might apply to our problem.
We could consult guides that bucket the problem into "relationship," "comparison," "distribution," "composition," and so on. But those things are still too vague and leave us with dozens of options to try, all of which can be pretty generic.

The problem

menus become rules

This chart is good. This chart is bad. Use this color scheme, not that one.

Rules are useful, but brittle. They tend to be all-or-nothing just when scientific graphics need judgment.

This is the SciPy framing. I am not arguing against heuristics; I am arguing that chart typologies should not be the only way we see a figure.

The stance

I'm a stylistic anarchist

Not anti-style. Anti brittle rules.

A prerequisite to designing good graphics is seeing what they are doing as clearly as possible, before ranking them by type.

Direct perception and non-judgmental awareness. First name what the graphic asks the eye to do, then make the judgment call.

A better lens

grammar vs elements of style

Grammar names the relationships. Style controls the voice.

Grammar

containment, alignment, connection, order, scale, emphasis

Style

palette, texture, typography, density, ornament, tone

This keeps style tips from undercutting the message. The point is not that style is unimportant; it is that style decisions should answer structural and perceptual questions.

graphics don't always fall into neat taxonomies

Key idea: when we design a graphic, we're doing more than picking a chart type. We're creating a visual structure for our readers to navigate.
data structure → visual structure → reader
The key idea of this talk is that when we're designing a graphic, we're doing a lot more than just picking a chart type — we're creating a visual structure for our readers to navigate. The way we arrange shapes and the colors we assign affect what our readers see first, what comparisons they make, and what story they take away.

In this talk you're going to see many examples of mapping data structures to visual structures to reader experiences. And through that process I'm gonna show you how thinking structurally frees you from this useful, but rigid lens of chart types and black-and-white design rules like banning pie charts, and helps you think more precisely and creatively about visualization.

To talk about visual structure precisely, we need a language.

GoFish
A compositional language for visual structure
open source (MIT-licensed), TypeScript with Python bindings (quickly catching up to TS)
See papers: UIST 2024, VIS 2025
And to talk about visual structure precisely, we're gonna want a language. I've spent my PhD studying how visual structure conveys meaning and designing better libraries to take advantage of it.

The culmination of that work is GoFish, a compositional language for visual structure.

GoFish is based on Gestalt grouping principles

containment
connection
similar attribute
alignment
proximity / uniform density
The big idea in GoFish is to identify these primitives of visual structure, most often called Gestalt grouping principles, and turn them in "graphical operators" that we can manipulate directly in code. In fact with the grouped bar charts, similar attribute and spatial proximity were driving the differences in our readings of those charts. They made different visual "queries" easier or harder.

connection
similar attribute
alignment
proximity / uniform density
We can see this play out in a more complex graphic, too. Containment is suggested by the axes in each of the charts here, connection is used for labeling, similar attribute to group points and parts of the diagrams together, and so on.

derive() to sort

chart(barley)
  .flow(
    spread({ by: "variety", dir: "x", spacing: 24 }),
    spread({ by: "year", dir: "x", spacing: 4 }),
    derive(d => orderBy(d, "yield", "asc")),
    stack({ by: "site", dir: "y" })
  )
  .mark(
    rect({ h: "yield", fill: "site" })
  )

Add year as a second grouping, then sort each stack by yield before placing it.

Derive is a data transform, blue. Because construction is split into explicit steps, we can insert a sort between spreading and stacking.

connect related bars

Layer([
  chart(barley)
    .flow(
      spread({ by: "variety", dir: "x", spacing: 38 }),
      spread({ by: "year", dir: "x", spacing: 8 }),
      derive(d => orderBy(d, "yield", "asc")),
      stack({ by: "site", dir: "y" })
    )
    .mark(rect({ w: 16, h: "yield", fill: "site" }).name("bars")),
  chart(selectAll("bars"))
    .flow(group({ by: "variety" }), group({ by: "site" }))
    .mark(area({ opacity: 0.5 }))
])
The bars remain available as named structure; the second layer connects across them. Key: we didn't throw away the stacked structure, we added continuity on top of it.

chart(scatter_data).flow(

Outer structure: scatter() affords "where?" queries.

Inner structure: pie affords "what fraction?" queries.

Queries:

  • which species dominates each lake? (largest slice)
  • how does species composition vary across geography?
  • which lakes have similar mixes? (visual comparison)
This is composition — nesting one query-affording structure inside another. It's the same idea as faceting: spreading a chart-making function. Pie affords proportion queries easily; magnitude queries are hard because arc angle is a weaker encoding than length. Cross-lake magnitude comparison is impossible because pies are normalized. ~18 min mark.

This is the famous Minnesota barley anomaly (Fisher/Immer data, popularized by Cleveland & via the trellis-display literature): nearly every site's yield fell from 1931 to 1932, except Morris, which rose. The pies sit at each site's actual geography — Crookston in the far northwest, Duluth on Lake Superior in the northeast, Waseca in the south, Morris mid-west — so the chart doubles as a map of Minnesota. Encoding total yield as pie radius (instead of a fixed radius, as in the lake version) makes the flip visible at a glance without giving up the proportion read inside each pie. Same outer/inner grammar as the seafood scatter-pie — only the radius channel is new.

What just happened?

We took one dataset and made a series of deliberate structural moves.

Each move changed what was easy or hard to see.

We didn't have to pick from a menu of charts... though those are useful shorthands for common structures!

Instead, chart type labels like bar or pie were just structural motifs we encountered along the way.

Synthesis after the scatter-pie → flower → balloon sequence (and the bar→polar arc before it). Let this breathe. ~24 min mark.

operators map data types to Gestalt relations

Operator Data type Gestalt relation
spread discrete alignment & uniform density
stack partitioned continuous part-to-whole
scatter continuous × continuous none — no discrete grouping
area / line / ribbon continuous or contiguous connection
table discrete × discrete uniform density
Each operator corresponds to a specific Gestalt principle. Spread creates alignment and uniform density across discrete categories. Stack creates part-to-whole contiguity. Area/line/ribbon use element connectedness to imply continuity. Scatter deliberately avoids a discrete grouping — proximity is the only cue. Table and align/distribute are constraint-based; their Gestalt relation is also alignment and density, but the mechanism differs.

takeaways

1. Chart types are great shortcuts, but graphics are really sophisticated visual structures.

2. Changing this structure changes what your reader can and can't see in (probably) predictable ways.

3. Thinking structurally helps us move beyond familiar charts and rigid rules to designs that might better fit our data or task.

Land all three explicitly. 1: the Part 1 journey — bar, stacked, sorted, ribbon, polar — weren't different chart types, they were structural moves. 2: affordances thread — every move opens some queries and closes others. 3: the composition payoff — scatter-pie, flower, balloon weren't in any dropdown. Then the honest close: GoFish is one attempt, Q&A is needfinding. ~27 min mark.
