The second answer: we could consult guides that bucket the problem into "relationship," "comparison," "distribution," "composition," and so on. But those buckets are still too vague to decide anything. For one, they're hard to tell apart, what exactly is the difference between a comparison, a relationship, and a composition? the grammar of graphics: marks and channels

marks (shapes)

Observable Plot

channels (shape attributes)

Munzner, Visualization Analysis and Design (2014)

but there's no account of structure, so it can't explain what we just saw

This is the strongest answer yet. You might object that the grammar of graphics already gives us first principles: marks and channels. Marks are the shapes; channels are how data maps onto them, and there's a famous accuracy ranking: position and length beat angle, area, and color. That tells us how precisely one value can be read. But you just saw the counterexample: same marks, same channels, same rankings, and you read the three charts differently. Marks and channels are blind to arrangement and how things are grouped and laid out. So we need a way of talking about arrangement.

Marks and channels tell us how precisely to read a value. They don't tell us which questions a chart makes easy.

Key idea: when we design a graphic, we're doing more than picking a chart type. We're creating a visual structure for our readers to navigate.

GoFish is based on Gestalt grouping principles

containment
connection
similar attribute
alignment
proximity / uniform density
The big idea in GoFish is to identify these primitives of visual structure, most often called Gestalt grouping principles, and turn them in "graphical operators" that we can manipulate directly in code. In fact with the grouped bar charts, similar attribute and spatial proximity were driving the differences in our readings of those charts. They made different visual "queries" easier or harder.
