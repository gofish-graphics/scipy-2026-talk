You've probably seen the grammar of graphics, or marks and channels. Marks are the shapes; channels are how data maps onto them, and there's a famous accuracy ranking: position and length beat angle, area, and color. That tells us how precisely one value can be read. But both of our charts used the same marks and the same channels, so the ranking can't explain why one grouping made the trend pop and the other hid it. Hold that question.

Marks and channels tell us how precisely to read a value. They don't tell us which questions a chart makes easy.

The key idea of this talk is that when we're designing a graphic, we're doing a lot more than just picking a chart type — we're creating a visual structure for our readers to navigate. The way we arrange shapes and the colors we assign affect what our readers see first, what comparisons they make, and what story they take away.

In this talk you're going to see many examples of mapping data structures to visual structures to reader experiences. And through that process I'm gonna show you how thinking structurally frees you from this useful, but rigid lens of chart types and black-and-white design rules like banning pie charts, and helps you think more precisely and creatively about visualization.

The big idea in GoFish is to identify these primitives of visual structure, most often called Gestalt grouping principles, and turn them in "graphical operators" that we can manipulate directly in code. In fact with the grouped bar charts, similar attribute and spatial proximity were driving the differences in our readings of those charts. They made different visual "queries" easier or harder.

the plan doesn't forbid queries — it prices them

swap the plan, and the cheap query swaps with it

Now we can say precisely what happened at the start of the talk. The orange chip is the plan the chart fixes: two nested for-eaches (the scan order) plus the case block of encodings. The blue chip under each column is the query that plan makes cheap. On the left, cities sit adjacent inside each year block, so comparing sales across cities with the year held fixed is one glance; on the right, each city's years sit adjacent, so comparing sales across years with the city held fixed is the easy one. In each case the other comparison turns into a scan across a group boundary. And the "held fixed" conditioning language is deliberate — it foreshadows the Simpson's paradox slide later in the deck, where what you hold fixed changes the answer. But the faded chips matter just as much: neither plan forbids the other query. The second encode mapping — city → color — is precisely what keeps the cross-group comparison alive. On the left, a city's bars are scattered across the year blocks and only their color tracks them; on the right, color confirms identity across the long scan between groups. Structure prices queries rather than forbidding them: adjacency makes one cheap, color keeps the other admissible. These chips are perceptual query plans, not just queries: the two orderings are logically the same question, but perceptually different plans — like a database EXPLAIN, order is the whole point.
