three stacks make a mosaic

const share = field("count").normalize()

chart(titanic)
  .flow(
    stack({ by: "class",    dir: "y", size: share }),
    stack({ by: "sex",      dir: "x", size: share }),
    stack({ by: "survived", dir: "y", size: share })
  )
  .mark(
    rect({ fill: "survived" })
  )

No new operators — just stack three deep. Cell area is the joint count; the blue fraction is the survival rate. And the nesting order decides which comparison you read first — the same choice as the grouped bars that opened the talk.

Titanic passengers: class, sex, survived. Three stacks, nested, alternating direction, each normalized within its parent scope. Read it: within every class, women survived at higher rates — "women and children first" as one uniform visual sentence — and survival falls from first class to third within each sex. Note this is the Charlie-and-River move again: swapping the nesting order changes whether you read "survival by sex, within each class" or "survival by class, within each sex." A mosaic isn't in the chart-type dropdown of most tools; here it's the operator we already know, composed one level deeper.

the same moves, stranger structures

mosaic stack ∘ stack ∘ stack

sankey tree spread + stack + ribbon

rose stack, in clock() coords

sunburst tree(...), in polar() coords

orthogonal tree tree(...) with elbow links

bottle pictorial layer + paint + constraints

None of these is a "chart type." You already know every move on this screen.

The same structural moves that took us from bars to a heatmap also build mosaics, sankeys, trees, and pictorials — forms no chart-type menu contains. [advance] And the operator captions are the point: the mosaic is three stacks; the sankey is spreads and stacks with ribbons connecting named tiers; the rose is the stacked bar wrapped into clock coordinates — polar is a coordinate change on structure we already built, not a new chart family. The sunburst and the orthogonal tree come from GoTree, a tree-layout grammar embedded in GoFish; the bottle composites an image with a fill bar through a paint blend mode and aligns its label with constraints. Structure, not taxonomy, is the through-line.

diagrams are visual data structures too

"We have three pulleys, two weights, and some ropes, arranged as follows:

  1. The first weight is suspended from the left end of a rope over Pulley A. The right end of this rope is attached to, and partially supports, the second weight.
  2. Pulley A is suspended from the left end of a rope that runs over Pulley B, and under Pulley C. Pulley B is suspended from the ceiling. The right end of the rope that runs under Pulley C is attached to the ceiling.
  3. Pulley C is attached to the second weight, supporting it jointly with the right end of the first rope.

[…] Find the ratio of the second to the first weight, if the system is in equilibrium."

Larkin & Simon 1987, "Why a Diagram Is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words"

look up Pulley A → which ropes pull on it?
Larkin and Simon's classic. The paragraph and the diagram carry exactly the same information — they are informationally equivalent — but they are not computationally equivalent. The diagram indexes the facts by location: everything you need about Pulley A is AT Pulley A, so the query is one glance, while the paragraph makes you search and join clauses. In our terms: the diagram is the better data structure for the queries the physics asks. And this diagram is not clip art — it's the GoFish port of Bluefish's pulley example, built from the same layers, alignment, and constraints as everything else today. The trees on the previous slide, this pulley: the operators don't stop at charts.

Why "more"

a grammar of more graphics

The Grammar of Graphics fits inside GoFish — and so do grammars beyond it.

GoFish's own test gallery reimplements, example by example:

  • vega-lite — the grammar underneath Altair
  • Atom — unit & dot-plot charts
  • PiCCL — pictorial charts (the bottle)
  • GoTree — a tree-layout grammar, 46 gallery examples
  • seaborn-style jointplots, and Bluefish diagrams
This is the sense of "more." These aren't screenshots of other tools — each of these grammars exists as a folder of working examples in GoFish's own test suite. The vega-lite gallery is the engine underneath Altair, so the charts Altair makes easy stay easy. And the grammars Altair and matplotlib can't reach — unit charts, pictorials, tree layouts, diagrams — are the same operators composing further, not custom SVG code. The standard grammar is a subset; the composition keeps going where the chart-type menu stops.

takeaways

1. Chart types are great shortcuts, but graphics are really sophisticated visual structures.

2. Changing this structure changes what your reader can and can't see in (probably) predictable ways.

3. Thinking structurally helps us move beyond familiar charts and rigid rules to designs that might better fit our data or task.

Land all three explicitly. 1: the Part 1 journey — bar, stacked, sorted, ribbon, polar — weren't different chart types, they were structural moves. 2: affordances thread — every move opens some queries and closes others. 3: the composition payoff — scatter-pie, flower, balloon weren't in any dropdown.
