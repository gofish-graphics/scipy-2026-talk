First move, in two steps. All three charts carry the same per-lake species shares. Bars to pies is stack-to-clock coordinates — note the chips are identical, because that step is a pure restyle. Nothing gained, nothing lost (hold that thought for the last slide). chart(seafood)
  .flow(
    spread(by="lake", dir="x"),

First pair, same seafood data. The left sizes each segment continuously; the right chunks the same counts into unit squares. The nested for-eaches are identical — what changes is the last line: an eyeballed "share of the total" becomes an exact "count squares," because the units are countable atoms.

moves that change the query

move 2 of 3 · one more level

did third class fare better than crew?

compare ⟨survival shares⟩ across classes
→

add sex: the answer flips

compare ⟨survival shares⟩ across class × sex 
share = field("count").normalize()

chart(titanic)
  .flow(
    stack("class", dir="y", size=share),
        stack("sex", dir="x", size=share),
    stack("survived", dir="y", size=share)
  )
  .mark(rect(fill="survived"))

one level short, the chart says the opposite. (Simpson's paradox)

Second pair, Titanic data — and this one has teeth. The two-level mosaic answers the class question directly: Third class survived at 25.2%, Crew at 24.0%, so third class fared better. Add the sex level and the answer flips within BOTH sexes: among men, Crew 22.3% vs Third 17.3%; among women, Crew 87.0% vs Third 45.9%. The mechanism: the crew is 97% male, and men survived at far lower rates everywhere, so the crew's aggregate is dragged down below Third's. Simpson's paradox — the extra for-each isn't decoration, it changes the verdict. Next: one more move (connect, then sort) before the counterexample.

layer([
  chart(energyMix)
    .flow(spread(by="year", dir="x", spacing=30),
            stack(field("source").sort("amount"), dir="y", size="amount"))
    .mark(rect(w=36, fill="source").name("bars")),
    chart(select("bars")).flow(group(by="source")).mark(ribbon(opacity=0.5))
])

UK electricity generation · Our World in Data / Ember

Third move — a composition, so the layout shows the two ingredients separately and then combined. Left top: sort each year's stack by amount — rank reads as position, but sorting re-purposes position from an identity channel to a rank channel, so following one source across years is a color hunt. Left bottom: connect instead — the bands carry "same source" identity across years, but in fixed stacking order they never cross, so there's nothing to read at a crossing. Compose them and the gained clause appears: with rank as position AND identity as continuity, a crossing IS a rank inversion — an overtake event, read directly. Real data: UK share of electricity generation (Our World in Data / Ember). Wind passes Coal between 2014 and 2018 — Coal collapses 29.6% to 5.0%, so Nuclear passes it too — then Wind passes Nuclear between 2018 and 2022, and Solar passes Coal by 2022: renewables overtaking legacy sources one crossing at a time. Contrast with the barley walkthrough this replaced: there the story was sign-of-change (did each variety rise or fall?), and the ribbon answered an adjacent rank question — the slope panels carry that beat now. Rank change is the query ribbons are actually FOR, so the ribbon lives here.

Not every move is structural. three stacks make a mosaic

share = field("count").normalize()

chart(titanic)
  .flow(
    stack("class",    dir="y", size=share),
    stack("sex",      dir="x", size=share),
    stack("survived", dir="y", size=share)
  )
  .mark(
    rect(fill="survived")
  )

No new operators — just stack three deep. Cell area is the joint count; the blue fraction is the survival rate. And the nesting order decides which comparison you read first — the same choice as the grouped bars that opened the talk.

Titanic passengers: class, sex, survived. Three stacks, nested, alternating direction, each normalized within its parent scope. Read it: within every class, women survived at higher rates — "women and children first" as one uniform visual sentence — and survival falls from first class to third within each sex. Note this is the Charlie-and-River move again: swapping the nesting order changes whether you read "survival by sex, within each class" or "survival by class, within each sex." A mosaic isn't in the chart-type dropdown of most tools; here it's the operator we already know, composed one level deeper.

the same moves, stranger structures

sankey tree

pulley diagram

sunburst

orthogonal tree

Keep composing the same moves and you run out of chart names.

Escalation beat. The moves the audience just learned — spread, stack, nest, restyle — don't stop where the chart menu does. Walk the tiles and say the operators out loud, since they're not printed on the slide anymore: the sankey is spreads and stacks with ribbons connecting named tiers. The sunburst and the orthogonal tree come from GoTree, a tree-layout grammar embedded in GoFish. And the pulley diagram is not clip art: it's the GoFish docs-gallery export of the Bluefish pulley example, drawn by the same layers, alignment, and constraints as everything else in this talk. It reproduces Figure 1 of Larkin and Simon 1987, "Why a Diagram Is (Sometimes) Worth Ten Thousand Words" — their claim is this talk's thesis stated for diagrams in 1987: a diagram earns its keep by making the right inferences cheap. None of these four has a slot in a chart dropdown — they're all the same composition carried further. It's the escalation beat taken to its limit — the sankey, sunburst, and tree at least have chart names; the pulley has none. [advance] Keep composing the same moves and you don't just run out of chart names, you leave charts behind entirely. The structure language already reaches diagrams; the query language for them is open research — a diagram's queries are richer than for-each and share, things like incidence, attachment, which way does it turn, and that's where the research goes next. Close the loop: the strange Science figures and systems diagrams from the opening are in reach of the same moves. Why "more"

a grammar of more graphics

The Grammar of Graphics fits inside GoFish — and so do grammars beyond it.

GoFish's own test gallery reimplements, example by example:

  • vega-lite — the grammar underneath Altair
  • Atom — unit & dot-plot charts
  • PiCCL — pictorial charts (the bottle)
  • GoTree — a tree-layout grammar, 46 gallery examples
  • seaborn-style jointplots, and Bluefish diagrams
This is the sense of "more." These aren't screenshots of other tools — each of these grammars exists as a folder of working examples in GoFish's own test suite. The vega-lite gallery is the engine underneath Altair, so the charts Altair makes easy stay easy. And the grammars Altair and matplotlib can't reach — unit charts, pictorials, tree layouts, diagrams — are the same operators composing further, not custom SVG code. The standard grammar is a subset; the composition keeps going where the chart-type menu stops.

takeaways

1. Chart types are great shortcuts, but graphics are really sophisticated visual structures.

2. Changing this structure changes what your reader can and can't see in (probably) predictable ways.

3. Thinking structurally helps us move beyond familiar charts and rigid rules to designs that might better fit our data or task.

Land all three explicitly. 1: the Part 1 journey — bar, stacked, sorted, ribbon, polar — weren't different chart types, they were structural moves. 2: affordances thread — every move opens some queries and closes others. 3: the composition payoff — scatter-pie, flower, balloon weren't in any dropdown. Then the honest close: GoFish is one attempt, Q&A is needfinding. ~27 min mark.
