anchor the comparison

One edit: normalize each variety's 1931 yield to zero, so the line's own angle becomes the delta. 

chart(barley)  .flow(    derive(anchor_to_1931),    spread(by="site", dir="x"),    spread(by="year", dir="x"),    scatter(by="variety", y="delta_from_1931"),  )  .mark(line(stroke="variety"))
One edit from the last slide: `derive` computes each variety's delta from its own 1931 yield, and `y` is rebound to that delta instead of raw yield. Every line still starts at the same baseline (zero), so nothing about "up is more" is lost — the slope metaphor survives intact. What's new is that the line's ANGLE now IS the delta, not just a byproduct of two absolute values. Nothing is thrown away yet; this step only compresses information that was already implicit in the previous chart.

the line has nothing left to connect

find ⟨the sign of Δ yield⟩, by site the whole investigation, compiled to one query

One edit: derive collapses the year pair into a single delta. The line isn't removed for taste — it becomes ungrammatical. 

chart(barley)  .flow(    derive(delta),    spread(by="site", dir="x"),    spread(by="variety", dir="x"),  )  .mark(rect(h="delta"))
The key beat of the whole arc. `derive` now folds each variety's two years into one delta, so the year spread has nothing left to spread over — it's gone, and `variety` takes its place as the inner facet. Once each (site, variety) pair is down to a single value, there is no second point left for a line to connect — the mark isn't swapped from `line` to `rect` because bars look nicer, it's swapped because a line needs two endpoints and this data only has one. The data itself took the line away. A signed bar from a zero baseline keeps the up-is-more read from the slope, just compressed onto one axis.

Same beat as the bars slide: derive collapses the year pair, so there is nothing left for a line to connect. queries after Brehmer & Munzner (2013)
spread — discrete; alignment & uniform density.
stack — partitioned continuous; part-to-whole.
scatter — continuous × continuous; proximity.
area / line / ribbon — continuous or contiguous; connection.
table — discrete × discrete; uniform density.

Every operator does two jobs: an addressing move (what's where, what's locatable) and a pricing move (which readings become one perceptual act). Connection is the only pure pricing move — and the scatterpie works by keeping scatter's addressing while swapping the value payload.

The scatterpie composes two of these: scatter contributes how the distribution varies across space, the pie glyph contributes share-within-entity.

Connection is the strongest grouping cue there is — stronger than proximity, stronger than color or shape similarity (Franconeri, Bemis, Burlingham & Kramer 2021, PSPI, Fig. 8). Explicit connecting lines improve relation judgments by 25–95% (Nothelfer & Franconeri 2020). Note this is a strength ranking, not Palmer & Rock's 1994 claim about processing priority (which is about order of computation, not which cue wins).

That ranking is a default, not a total order: connection > common region/connectedness > proximity > similarity (color > shape). Cue strength is parametric and cues combine additively in log-odds (Kubovy & van den Berg 2008; Brooks 2015), so a strong enough similarity cue can override a weak proximity cue (Quinlan & Wilton 1998). In grouped bar charts specifically, spatial proximity beats color grouping for comparisons about 63/37 of the time (Bearfield, Stokes, Lovett & Franconeri 2024, TVCG). Full notes: notes/gestalt-grouping-strength.md.

The bottom line about color is the nonlocal grouping cue: similarity groups marks without adjacency and without building a spatial index — no by= to point to. By default it sits below proximity in the strength ranking above, but that ordering is parametric, not fixed: a large enough color difference, or tight enough spacing, can flip it (same Quinlan & Wilton 1998 / Bearfield et al. 2024 point as above, applied to this table's own operators).

the ask column's verbs are Brehmer & Munzner's: look up and follow are their search actions, compare/read/collect their query actions. Their identify/compare/summarize ladder maps onto this table three ways: identify is a mark-level read, compare is the operator's ask line, summarize is the hole's own collapsed-chip gloss.

spread is align + distribute: distribute builds the index (the index cell — one slot per key), align prices the comparison itself (the ask line). Which align anchor you pick decides which comparison is precise: baseline anchoring gives common-scale length comparisons, middle anchoring gives symmetric deviation comparisons.

stack trades per-key comparison accuracy — interior segments lose the shared baseline the moment they're not first — for a free read of the total at the far edge. That far edge, Σ size, is itself a size an enclosing operator can consume. Normalize it (size: field(...).normalize()) and the far edge reads as ⟨each key's share⟩ instead of an absolute total.

scatter's key is one field, with x/y carried as attributes (by= is optional — omit it for a per-item scatter). A 1D scatter, with only x or only y bound, is a strip plot — hence "1–2 axes" under the preview. table's key is two fields crossed (by={row, col}); that row/col split is what makes it discrete both ways, versus scatter's continuous one-or-two.

⟨the shape of the crowd⟩ = trends, clusters, outliers — the gestalt reads a scatter affords for free. Scagnostics is the formalization of exactly that list.

What each operator's "places" used to say, before the shape column replaced the prose: spread — one slot per value of a field. stack — scopes tiled end to end; boundaries at the running totals. scatter — each entity at its location in space. area / line / ribbon — nothing new; draws over existing placements. table — two independently addressable axes.
