each line of the spec is a cut

Highlighting a line of the spec picks a CUT through the operator tree — Elmqvist-style hierarchical aggregation, but the cut is wherever your eye lands in the code, not a fixed level someone baked into the chart. Below the cut, the tree collapses into a summary glyph: on this first step, an entire site's spread over years is one neutral gray bar of its total, because collapsing IS just an inner substitution GoFish already does whenever a grouping level is left out of the spec — the same auto-summing behind "back to those bar charts." So the visualization of the idea (fade what's below the cut into a summary) is itself an instance of the idea (a lower level of the spec, collapsed). Above the cut, everything stays present, and the focused region is marked by a drawn dashed boundary around the bars in play rather than by graying out the rest — this slide has earned the right to use its own machinery: common region is the strong Gestalt cue (the whole reason grouping/adjacency reads as comparison at all), where dimming was only ever a salience nudge borrowed for the earlier Boulder slide back in the opening. Watch the boundary as the steps advance: it starts around the entire plot (every site still participates, nothing pinned down yet) and shrinks one enclosure at a time, down to Morris's pair of bars, down to a single bar. The chip states that same shrinking directly — a context header of concrete field = value bindings for ONE example region (site = Morris; site = Morris, year = 1932) instead of the abstract "held fixed" language the old chips used — so the header grows exactly as the boundary shrinks: two views of the same narrowing cut. Below the header, the query body has a hole — ⟨each site's year pattern⟩, ⟨yield heights⟩ — for what the boundary is now cheap to compare, and the gloss line underneath is the cost of actually running that query in this arrangement: first the perceptual act the layout affords (groups sitting apart, bars sitting adjacent), then what that act costs the reader (a scan, one glance). This is also the perceptual-query notation itself, introduced properly for the first time: the dashed hole is a placeholder for the reader's own attention, the gloss is a cost estimate, not a verdict.

every hole is a collapsed chip

compare ⟨?each site's year pattern⟩ across sites groups sit apart — a scan
site = Morris compare ⟨?yield heights⟩ across years
site = Morris · year = 1932 yield → height · site → color
Docking is evaluation, not substitution. A chip is a query — an act you can run when you attend inside a region; what crosses the boundary upward is only the act's export. When the mark chip docks into the year chip, its whole body collapses to ⟨yield heights⟩ — and notice the header: C's binding year = 1932 is discharged by B's "across years", just as B's site = Morris is discharged by A's "across sites". Headers shrink as you dock upward; on the cut slides they grew as we cut downward — same interface, two directions. The mapping chip-to-summary is many-to-one: a stacked bar and a waffle are different chips exporting the same summary type at different precision, which is exactly why inner substitution preserves the outer query. This is also the magnification and screen-reader result: zooming out of a region replaces the region's query with its export. Scratch's reporter blocks are the visual ancestor, but with collapse-on-dock: the fully nested one-liner exists only mid-flight; the resting state is always collapsed.

this time, start from the query — then build the structure that answers it

The totals rose at Morris and fell everywhere else. But a total can hide its parts: maybe one big variety swung it. To answer, we break each site open by variety. New move: instead of reading the query off a chart we already built, write the query down first — for each variety, for each site, how did yield change — and go hunting for the structure that makes it cheap. The rest of this section is that hunt.

stack()

read ⟨total yield across sites⟩ for each variety × year a cheaper query than the Δ one — worth trying first 
chart(barley)
  .flow(
    spread(by="variety", dir="x"),
    spread(by="year", dir="x"),
    stack("site", dir="y", size="yield")
  )
  .mark(
    rect(w=8, fill="site")
  )

The first thing to try: totals and the rough site mix become cheap reads. But the Δ query stays expensive — segments sit on shifting baselines. Velvet at Grand Rapids rose +9.2 bu/acre, the largest rise outside Morris, and you can barely see it move.

Stack is a layout operator, green. It's the natural first move on the Δ query, and it partially pays off: total yield per variety and the rough site mix both become cheap reads. But it prices per-part change out. Once a segment sits above others in the stack, its two endpoints (1931 stack, 1932 stack) share a shifting baseline set by every segment below it, so the eye can't isolate its own Δ. Concretely: Velvet at Grand Rapids rose +9.2 bushels/acre, the biggest jump anywhere outside Morris — bigger than Morris's own Manchuria (+6.9) or Glabron (+6.4) — and it's invisible here. That failure is the point: it's what sends us hunting for a structure built for comparing change directly, rather than reading it off two stacked totals. Slope panels and the Δ heatmap, later in the glyphs section, are that structure.

what does connecting things do?

Bars afford discrete comparisons; connected marks afford trends — even when the x-axis is categorical. Zacks & Tversky (1999)

Before we add the next operator, a perception question: what does connecting marks do? Zacks & Tversky (1999), "Bars and lines: a study of graphic communication," showed people the same data as bars and as a line. Bar readers produced discrete comparisons — "men are taller than women." Line readers produced trends — even on categorical data, giving absurd readings like "the more male a person is, the taller he or she is." Connection is not decoration: it asserts continuity and identity, and it licenses trend readings. Sorting a stack by value buys rank-as-position but gives up identity — "same site, same slot" is gone — and connection is exactly the operator that buys it back. (That trade plays out later, in the closing section's move-4 slide, on the energy ribbon.)

connect related bars

layer([
  chart(barley)
    .flow(
      spread(by="variety", dir="x", spacing=24),
      spread(by="year", dir="x", spacing=16),
      stack(field("site").sort("yield"), dir="y", size="yield")
    )
    .mark(rect(w=8, fill="site").name("bars")),
  chart(selectAll("bars"))
    .flow(group(by="variety"), group(by="site"))
    .mark(ribbon(opacity=0.5))
])

Connection restores the identity that sorting gave up: the areas track each site across stacks, and a crossing reads as a rank change.

The bars remain available as named structure; the second layer connects across them. Key: we didn't throw away the stacked structure, we added continuity on top of it. This is the connection affordance from the last slide put to work: the areas assert "same site" across the sorted stacks, so tracking is a trend read instead of a color hunt, and a crossing is a rank change you see directly.
