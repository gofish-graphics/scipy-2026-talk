Next, three slides walk that same spec bottom-up, starting from a single mark and moving the cut up the spec one line at a time, tying each line to a perceptual-query chip, the notation promised back in the opening.

Highlighting a line of the spec picks a CUT through the operator tree, Elmqvist-style hierarchical aggregation, but the cut is wherever your eye lands in the code, not a fixed level someone baked into the chart. We start at the very bottom of the spec, the mark line, so the cut sits as low as it can go: the boundary is a single bar, and there is nothing below the cut left to collapse into a summary glyph, because the mark is the leaf, this first step is the one place in the whole sequence where nothing is hidden. The focused region is marked by a drawn dashed boundary around the bar in play rather than by graying out the rest, this slide has earned the right to use its own machinery: common region is the strong Gestalt cue (the whole reason grouping/adjacency reads as comparison at all), where dimming was only ever a salience nudge borrowed for the earlier Boulder slide back in the opening. Watch the boundary as the steps advance: right now it hugs a single bar, as small as it can get, and it will grow one enclosure at a time as the cut moves up the spec, first to Morris's pair of bars, then to the entire plot. The chip states that same story directly: the header is a full set of concrete field = value bindings (site = Morris, year = 1932), every coordinate is pinned down, so there's no hole in the query body, just a direct channel mapping, yield → height, site → color. With nothing left to search for and nothing to aggregate, the query degrades to the cheapest thing on the menu: a lookup. As the cut moves up from here, a binding will leave the header, a hole will open where it used to be, and that hole is what the next slide's notation is for. The chip that just landed isn't alone on the slide by accident: it's the bottom of a column, and that column will grow upward one chip at a time as the cut climbs the spec, so that by the top step every line of code has its chip sitting beside it at once.

Concretely, on this step it happens site by site: every site other than Morris has its whole spread over years flattened down to one neutral gray bar of its total, while Morris, the one site the header pins down, stays exploded into its own bar per year, because that's what's inside the boundary now. The boundary grows to match: from a single bar up to Morris's whole pair of bars, one enclosure bigger than the mark step. The header shrinks the same amount it grew: year = 1932 is gone, discharged into the query body as "across years," and only site = Morris is left pinned down, boundary and header are the same widening cut, drawn vs. written. This is also where the perceptual-query notation is introduced properly for the first time: the hole in the chip, ⟨yield heights⟩, is a placeholder for the reader's own attention, marking what the boundary is now cheap enough to compare but hasn't been read out yet. search, where is it? look up find follow look up: you were handed the address. find: you weren't, the chart has to make the answer visible. follow: trace a link. query, what about it? read compare collect read: decode one value or event. compare: relate two or more located things. collect: summarize all of them.
One more line up, and we're at the top of the spec: the cut moves onto the outer spread(by="site"), and now the year line collapses too, along with the mark line beneath it, both fall below the cut, both fold into summary glyphs wherever the layout doesn't need them expanded. The boundary takes its last, biggest step: from Morris's pair of bars out to the entire plot, every site now participating, nothing held back as a special case anymore. The header shrinks to match, and this time it empties out completely, there's no field = value binding left to write, because we're no longer looking at one example region, we're looking at all of them at once. That's exactly what the query body says without any header at all: ⟨each site's year pattern⟩, compared across sites. All three chips are up now, and the column next to the spec is the whole claim of this slide's title made literal: read down the column and you're reading down the spec, one chip per line, in the same order. Read it top to bottom and two things move in opposite directions, the headers thin out as you go up, a full site = Morris · year = 1932 address at the bottom, down to site = Morris with year gone, down to no header at all at the top, while the holes do the opposite, growing from nothing, to a hole, to a whole shape. And the cost tracks both: a lookup at the bottom, a glance in the middle, a scan at the top. Three chips, three prices, one spec. The hole is bigger than either of the last two steps' holes, it's not one number, it's a whole shape, repeated once per site, and it's worth pricing that out loud: groups sitting apart is a real perceptual act, but reading ten of them side by side is a scan, the most expensive query in this three-step arc. Lookup, then one glance, then a scan, the query gets more expensive exactly as the boundary gets bigger and the header gets emptier; three prices for the same widening cut. The key underneath the chip spells out the six verbs the chips use, and they aren't arbitrary, they split into two tiers. Look up, find, and follow are all "where is it" questions: look up means someone handed you the address (a key, a coordinate) and the index just retrieves it; find means there is no address, you're hunting, and the chart has to make the answer visible; follow means tracing a link, like a line or a ribbon. Read, compare, and collect are all "what about it" questions once you're already looking at the right place: read decodes one value, compare relates two or more things you've found, collect summarizes the whole crowd at once. Worth saying out loud later, when the delta-bars slides come up: that whole four-step arc is the story of turning a find into something close to a look up. Early on, "which way did yield move at this site" is a real hunt, you have to read a line's slope, one of ten per panel, and there's no shortcut. By the time the bars are diverging from a zero baseline, the sign has an address: which side of the baseline the bar falls on. The audience isn't searching anymore, they're just looking. Same query the whole way through, priced cheaper and cheaper.

is it one variety, or all of them?

site = Morris compare ⟨each variety's yield⟩ across years

this time, start from the query, then build the structure that answers it

The totals rose at Morris and fell everywhere else. But a total can hide its parts: maybe one big variety swung it. To answer, we break each site open by variety. New move: instead of reading the query off a chart we already built, write the query down first, for each variety, for each site, how did yield change, and go hunting for the structure that makes it cheap. The rest of this section is that hunt, and the obvious first stop is the operator that just handed us site and year: spread again.

just spread again?

site = Morris compare ⟨each variety's yield⟩ across years 
chart(barley)
  .flow(
    spread(by="site", dir="x"),
    spread(by="variety", dir="x"),
    spread(by="year", dir="x")
  )
  .mark(
    rect(h="yield", fill="variety")
  )

Make variety the comparison unit, then put its two years side by side. The pairing is now right. But every change is still encoded as two separate heights, so the eye must infer ten differences within every site.

This is the strongest version of the plain spread approach. We wrote the query down, so make variety the unit of comparison inside each site, then place 1931 and 1932 next to each other inside each variety. That fixes the avoidable grouping problem: the two values whose difference we care about are now adjacent. What remains is the representational cost of bars themselves. Each change is still something we infer from two tiny heights, repeated ten times per site. The next slide turns each pair into a single connected trajectory, so direction becomes visible.

stack()

site = Morris compare ⟨each variety's yield⟩ across years 
chart(barley)
  .flow(
    spread(by="site", dir="x"),
    spread(by="year", dir="x"),
    stack("variety", dir="y", size="yield")
  )
  .mark(
    rect(w=8, fill="variety")
  )

Spreading a third time lost the total. Stacking variety instead of spreading it puts the parts back into a whole: opening each site's bar by variety shows that the total moved, bar heights differ between the two years. Whether the mix changed is still an expensive read: each variety's segment sits on a shifting baseline set by everything stacked below it.

Stack is a layout operator, green. Spreading by site and year first, then stacking variety within each site-year bar, answers the totals question for free: bar heights visibly differ between 1931 and 1932 at every site, the total that the third `spread` just threw away is back. But it prices the mix question out. Once a variety's segment sits above others in the stack, its two endpoints (1931 stack, 1932 stack) share a shifting baseline set by every segment below it, so the eye can't isolate whether that one variety's share changed or the whole stack just slid. Concretely: Velvet at Grand Rapids rose +9.2 bushels/acre, the biggest jump anywhere outside Morris, bigger than Morris's own Manchuria (+6.9) or Glabron (+6.4), and its segment is nearly impossible to isolate here, buried mid-stack on a baseline that itself moved. That failure is the point: it's what sends us to normalize next, throwing the total away on purpose to make the mix question cheap.

did the mix change?

site = Morris compare ⟨each variety's share of yield⟩ across years 
chart(barley)
  .flow(
    spread(by="site", dir="x"),
    spread(by="year", dir="x"),
    stack("variety", dir="y", size=field("yield").normalize())
  )
  .mark(
    rect(w=8, fill="variety")
  )

One line changed: normalize(). Every bar is now the same height, so the total is deliberately thrown away and only composition is left.

A negative result, and a real finding: the bands line up across each site's year pair, the variety mix held. It isn't one big variety swinging the total; it's all of them, moving together, roughly in proportion. Only the total moved. That's exactly what licenses the simplification that follows, if the mix is stable, the total (or really, its site-by-site delta) is the whole story, and we can stop carrying per-variety segments at all. The next beat abstracts down to a single number per site.

same question, as pies

site = Morris compare ⟨each variety's share of yield⟩ across years 
chart(barley)
  .flow(
    table(by="site", by="year"),
  )
  .mark(
    chart(coord=clock())
      .flow(stack("variety", dir="x", size="yield"))
      .mark(rect(fill="variety"))
  )

Still one line changed, and still the same normalized stack, only the coordinate system is different. Length became angle. The mix comparison that was a glance at the normalized bars (do the band boundaries line up?) is now a wedge-to-wedge comparison of angles, stacked one pie on top of another.

This is the exact same data, the exact same normalized stack, the exact same query, at Morris, did each variety's share change across the years?, with one thing swapped: length for angle. Same point as the length/angle pair earlier in the deck (pie vs. bar with five similar wedges): fine-grained comparison gets much harder. In the bar version, the mix question was answered by whether two horizontal band edges lined up, a single, cheap alignment judgment, repeated across six sites. Here, answering it means comparing a wedge's angle (or area) to the wedge directly below it, pie by pie, site by site, no shared baseline, no edge to align, just two shapes you have to hold in your head and compare. Nothing is wrong with the pies; they're honest and correctly normalized. They just price the same query much higher. That's the deck's whole thesis in miniature: the encoding should be chosen to make the query cheap, and here's a case where the "same" chart, one option changed, makes it dearer instead. Worth knowing for the Q&A but not worth putting on the slide: nesting a chart-as-glyph pie inside table() mislabels both axes (row ticks float above their row, column ticks bunch up instead of centering over their column), so this chart leans on two tick-realignment hacks (realignYearPieTicks, realignSitePieTicks) rather than hiding the bug.

Add year as a second grouping, then sort each stack by yield before placing it.

Derive is a data transform, blue. Because construction is split into explicit steps, we can insert a sort between spreading and stacking.

sort each stack

chart(barley)
  .flow(
    spread(by="variety", dir="x"),
    spread(by="year", dir="x"),
    stack(field("site").sort("yield"), dir="y", size="yield")
  )
  .mark(
    rect(w=8, fill="site")
  )

Order each stack by yield. Rank, min, and max now read off position; what we give up is the "same site, same slot" tracking.

Sorting is a modifier on the stacked field, not a separate step. It re-purposes position from an identity channel to a rank channel.

connect related bars

layer([
  chart(barley)
    .flow(
      spread(by="variety", dir="x", spacing=24),
      spread(by="year", dir="x", spacing=16),
      stack(field("site").sort("yield"), dir="y", size="yield")
    )
    .mark(rect(w=8, fill="site").name("bars")),
  chart(selectAll("bars"))
    .flow(group(by="variety"), group(by="site"))
    .mark(ribbon(opacity=0.5))
])

Connection restores the identity that sorting gave up: the areas track each site across stacks, and a crossing reads as a rank change.

The bars remain available as named structure; the second layer connects across them. Key: we didn't throw away the stacked structure, we added continuity on top of it. This is the connection affordance from the last slide put to work: the areas assert "same site" across the sorted stacks, so tracking is a trend read instead of a color hunt, and a crossing is a rank change you see directly.
